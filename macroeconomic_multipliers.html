Spending Multiplier Formula Derivation

Let's make the assumption that money spent by one person is another person's income

$$ \text{Money Spent by Person A } = \text{ Income of Person B} $$

Now let's assume that the marginal propensity to consume is the same for everyone throughout the entire economy, and that not everyone spends all their income.

The Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC) is the fraction of income an individual spends on consumer goods. It is always a value less than 1 in this model.

Since the GDP of a country relies on money spent on consumer goods, all the purchases are additive. For example $$ \text{Let I designate income by one person. So, the change in consumption of person 1 becomes } \Delta C_{1} = (MPC)I $$ Since this itself is another person's income, this can also be multiplied with MPC again $$ \Delta C_{2} = I{(MPC)}^{2} $$ This itself is also another person's income, so it can be multiplied by MPC once again. $$ \Delta C_{3} = I{(MPC)}^{3} $$ This can be repeated for n people in an economy: $$ \Delta C_{n} = I{(MPC)}^{n} $$ Now all these little changes in consumption can be added to contribute to the total change in Consumption spending in the economy $$ \Delta Y = \Delta C = \sum_{n=0}^{i} \Delta C_{n} = \sum_{n=0}^{i} I{(MPC)}^{n} $$ Now, let's assume that there are infinite rounds of spending or infinite transactions. So, i approaches infinity. $$ \Delta Y = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} I{(MPC)}^{n} = I\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} {(MPC)}^{n} $$ Since the initial income is constant, we can pull it out entirely outside the summation. Now, we are left with a geometric series that we must find the result for.

TIME TO SOLVE!!!!

We must use the nth term test to see if the series may diverge. $$ \lim_{n \to \infty} I {(MPC)}^{n} = 0 \text{ since } MPC < 1 $$ So, the nth term test is inconclusive because the result is zero. Now, let's use algebraic manipulation to find the geometric sum. $$ I\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} {(MPC)}^{n} = I\left(1 + (MPC) + (MPC)^{2} + (MPC)^{3} + ... + (MPC)^{n} \right) $$ Let's multiply both sides by the MPC $$ I(MPC)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} {(MPC)}^{n} = I\left((MPC) + (MPC)^{2} + (MPC)^{3} + (MPC)^{4} + ... + (MPC)^{n+1} \right) $$ We can subtract both equations from each other $$ I(1-MPC)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} {(MPC)}^{n} = I (1-(MPC)^{n+1}) $$ $$ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} {(MPC)}^{n} = \frac{1-(MPC)^{n+1}}{1-MPC} $$ Since we have the summation formula, we can take the limit as n goes to infinity $$ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1-(MPC)^{n+1}}{1-MPC} = \frac{1-0}{1-MPC} = \frac{1}{1-MPC} \text{ since } MPC < 1 $$ Therefore, the following sum becomes: $$ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} {(MPC)}^{n} = \frac{1}{1-MPC} $$ WE HAVE DERIVED THE FORMULA OF THE SPENDING MULTIPLIER! Now let's plug it back in to derive the change in GDP $$ \Delta Y = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} I{(MPC)}^{n} = \frac{I}{1-MPC} $$
