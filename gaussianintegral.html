Gaussian Integral

Here is the gaussian integral:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} dx$$

Let's solve it using Multivariable integration over Polar regions

$$\text{Let }I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} dx$$ Since I is defined in terms of x, we can also define it in terms of y $$I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-y^2} dy$$ Now let's multiply I to itself: $$ I^{2} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} dx * \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-y^2} dy = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2 - y^2} dx \wedge dy$$ We know that: $$ dA = dx \wedge dy $$ Now let's define dA for polar regions. We know that: $$ x = rcos(\theta) \quad{} y = r sin(\theta) \quad{} x^2 + y^2 = r^2 $$ Since the radius and theta are both changing, we have to differentiate both variables: $$ dx = cos(\theta)dr - r sin(\theta) d\theta \quad{} dy = sin(\theta)dr + rcos(\theta) d\theta$$ To multiply the two together, we have to use something called the wedge product: The wedge product is similar to the cross product and it is useful to multiply differentials together to result in a volume or area element $$ dx \wedge dy = (cos(\theta)dr - r sin(\theta) d\theta)\wedge(sin(\theta)dr + rcos(\theta) d\theta) $$ $$ dx \wedge dy = sin(\theta)cos(\theta) (dr \wedge dr) + r{cos}^{2} (\theta) dr d\theta - r{sin}^{2} (\theta) d\theta dr - sin(\theta)cos(\theta) {r}^{2} (d\theta \wedge d\theta)$$ Since the wedge product is like the cross product, it also follows the following property: $$ da \wedge db = - (db \wedge da) $$ Now, let's try to see what happens when we find the wedge product of the same differential: $$ da \wedge da = -(da \wedge da) \quad{} 2(da \wedge da) = 0 \quad{} \therefore da \wedge da = 0$$ So, the following terms become zero: $$ sin(\theta)cos(\theta) (dr \wedge dr) = 0 \quad{} - sin(\theta)cos(\theta) {r}^{2} (d\theta \wedge d\theta) = 0 $$ So, what we are left with is: $$ dx \wedge dy = r{cos}^{2} (\theta) (dr \wedge d\theta) - r{sin}^{2} (\theta) (d\theta \wedge dr) $$ The second terms can be rewritten as: $$ ( d\theta \wedge dr) = - (dr \wedge d\theta) $$ So, then it becomes: $$ dx \wedge dy = r{cos}^{2} (\theta) (dr \wedge d\theta) + r{sin}^{2} (\theta) (dr \wedge d\theta) = r (dr \wedge d\theta) $$ $$ \therefore dA = dx \wedge dy = r(dr \wedge d\theta) $$ Now we can plug this result back in: $$ I^{2} = \int\int e^{-r^2} r(dr \wedge d\theta)$$ Before solving this integral, we must define the bounds of the integral. $$ \text{Since the original bounds spanned all of } \mathbb{R}^2 \text{, we can imagine this region in polar terms as a circle.} $$ $$ \text{The circle spans all 360 degrees and spans from a radius of zero to an infinite radius} $$ $$ I^{2} = \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-r^2} r(dr \wedge d\theta)$$ We can use u-substitution for this now: $$ u = -r^2 \quad{} du = -2 r dr \quad{} rdr = -\frac{du}{2} \quad{} u(0) = 0 \quad{} u(\infty) = - \infty $$ $$ I^{2} = \frac{-1}{2} \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \int_{0}^{-\infty} e^u du \wedge d\theta = \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \int^{0}_{-\infty} e^u du \wedge d\theta = \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \left[ e^u \right]_{-\infty}^{0} d\theta $$ $$ \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2} \lim_{u \to -\infty} e^u = \frac{1}{2}-0 = \frac{1}{2} $$ $$ I^2 = \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{2 \pi} d\theta = \frac{1}{2} * 2 \pi = \pi $$ $$ I^2 = \pi $$ The result is strictly positive because the integrand in the original I is strictly positive. FINALLY, the result of the Gaussian Integral is: $$ I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} dx = \sqrt{\pi} $$
